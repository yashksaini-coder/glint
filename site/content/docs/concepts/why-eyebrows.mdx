Glint was designed around a long blink. It uses eyebrows because of what one recorded session showed.

## How it was measured

The free SDK tier is 50 minutes in total, so trial and error was not affordable. A prototype showed on-screen cues and wrote each one into the recording with a timestamp on the SDK's own clock. One session of about 2.5 minutes gave labelled examples of every gesture.

## What it showed

| Gesture | SDK signal | Face landmarks | Outcome |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Close eyes for 1 s, ×5 | `blinking` true in 1 of 4,096 frames | Eye openness never fell below 75% of normal | **Not detected** |
| Quick blink, ×5 | None | No dip | **Not detected** |
| Raise eyebrows, ×3 | `surprise` peaked at 76–84, only briefly | Smoothed ratio rose 31.6–33.5% | **Clear from landmarks** |
| Slow exhale, ×3 | Trace rose 2.4–7.9, then fell 6–15 | — | **Clear** |
| Talk for 10 s | `talking` in 81% of frames | — | Usable with averaging |
| Sit silent for 10 s | `talking` in 20% of frames | — | Too noisy to trust per frame |

The eyebrow signal itself separates the gestures cleanly. Blinks barely moved it, and raises went past the fire line.

## Why blinks failed

The person wore thick-framed glasses, which sit over the upper eyelid line, in a dim room with their face in shadow. That is the most likely reason the face model never saw a closed eye. An earlier session did record about 70 short blinks, none longer than 268 ms, so the blink signal does work in some conditions.

We cannot say that blinks never work. We can say they did not work in an ordinary setting, for a person wearing glasses, which is reason enough not to depend on them.

## What this does not prove

This is **one person, one session, three examples of each gesture.** The thresholds were then tuned on that same data, so the results are optimistic. Expect different numbers for different faces. Per-person [calibration](/docs/reference/roadmap) is the proper answer, and it is not built yet.
